## Thesis

For most of 2026 the Treasury curve has flattened while it sold off. The market priced a Fed that has to keep hiking into an energy shock, so short-dated yields rose faster than long-dated ones. I think that move has gone too far at both ends.

- **The front end prices more tightening than the Fed itself projects.** Hiring has slowed down significantly, and the committee starts to look less hawkish. I expect the Fed to deliver fewer hikes than the market prices.
- **The long end prices too little risk.** The Treasury and the AI build-out are both selling long-dated debt at record pace, the Fed is shrinking its balance sheet, Japan is buying fewer Treasuries, and political pressure on the Fed keeps long-run inflation risk alive. Investors should demand more compensation for holding 30-year bonds.

If both are right, the gap between 5-year and 30-year yields widens. That is the trade: a **5s30s steepener**, held for about three months, through the December FOMC.

> **Primer: reading the curve.** "5s30s" is the 30-year Treasury yield minus the 5-year yield, in basis points (bp; 1bp = 0.01%). A *steepener* profits when that gap widens; a *flattener* profits when it narrows. Traders also name the direction of the overall move. In a *bear steepener* all yields rise but the long end rises more, which is my main scenario. In a *bull steepener* all yields fall but the front end falls more, typically a growth scare where the Fed is expected to cut. A steepener makes money in both. It loses in a *bear flattener*, where the front end sells off fastest.

## What the market is pricing

**The Fed path.** The Fed hiked 25bp on 16 September to 3.75–4.00%, its first hike since 2023. The committee's median projection ("dot") is 4.125% at the end of both 2026 and 2027, implying one more hike then a hold. After a weak September jobs report, CME FedWatch put the odds of an October hike at only about 22%, but it prices three hikes in total by June 2027 (December fully priced, then March and June), taking the funds rate to 4.50–4.75%. That is about 50bp more tightening than the Fed's own projections.

![Market-implied fed funds path vs the Fed's median dot](/images/2026-10-fed-path-vs-dots.svg "The market prices a fed funds rate about 50bp above the Fed's median dot by mid-2027.")

**The curve.** On the 5 October close, the Treasury par curve stood at 4.84% for the 2-year, 5.06% for the 5-year, 5.31% for the 10-year and 5.66% for the 30-year. Since 27 February, the day before the Strait of Hormuz closed, the 5-year has risen 155bp but the 30-year only 102bp.

![Change in Treasury yields since 27 February, by maturity](/images/2026-10-yield-change-by-maturity.svg "The 5-year has led this year's sell-off.")

That gap is why 5s30s has narrowed this year, from about 113bp in February to a low of 41bp on 23 September. It has since bounced to 60bp.

![5s30s and 2s10s spreads through 2026](/images/2026-10-curve-slopes.svg "Both curve measures flattened through September; 5s30s fell further and has only partly recovered.")

**Term premium.** The New York Fed's ACM model estimates the 10-year term premium at 0.89% at the end of September, against a long-run average of about 1.43% since 1961.

> **Primer: term premium.** A 10-year yield is roughly the average overnight rate investors expect over the next ten years, *plus* a term premium, which is the extra yield demanded for locking money up for a decade and bearing the risk that inflation, supply or policy surprises along the way. The Fed's policy rate mostly drives the first part. Supply, demand for duration and uncertainty drive the second, and that is the part my long-end argument is about.

## Why the market could be wrong

**1. The front end is too hawkish for the data.** September payrolls rose just 29,000 against expectations of about 84,000. Unemployment ticked up to 4.2% and the prior two months were revised down. Average hourly earnings are up 3.0% from a year ago, which is not the kind of wage pressure that forces a central bank into a long series of hikes. The Fed's own dots already say one more hike and then hold. The market is betting the Fed will have to do more than it plans to; I think the labor market argues for less.

**2. Long-dated supply keeps growing.** The deficit is not shrinking. CBO projects a federal deficit of $1.9 trillion, or 5.8% of GDP, in fiscal 2026, rising to $3.1 trillion, or 6.7% of GDP, in 2036. On top of that, AI hyperscalers are funding data centers in the bond market. Wall Street estimates about $300 billion of AI-related investment-grade supply this year, much of it long-dated because data centers last decades. Long-dated corporate debt competes with long Treasuries for the same buyers of duration.

**3. The foreign buyer is stepping back.** Japan is the largest foreign holder of Treasuries, and its own yields now pay. The 10-year JGB briefly topped 3% in September, the first time since 1996, and Japanese demand for US long-term debt has turned negative this year. Net foreign purchases of Treasuries overall are at a post-2021 low.

**4. The Fed is shrinking its balance sheet, and its independence is under pressure.** Chair Warsh has said he prefers a smaller balance sheet over a higher funds rate as the main inflation tool. QT means the Fed is no longer absorbing duration, which pushes term premium up. Meanwhile the President keeps calling for rates of 1% or lower. A market that worries the Fed may eventually bend demands more inflation compensation in long bonds.

**5. The government has tried to hold the long end down, and so far the market has won.** In August, after the 30-year hit a 19-year high, Treasury doubled its buybacks of 10–30 year bonds to at least $4 billion per operation, funded by issuing more bills. Long yields fell, then erased the drop and rose above their prior level within a day. That is the single biggest risk to this trade, but it also shows how strong the selling pressure is.

Put together: the front end prices a Fed that does more than it says, and the long end prices less risk than its supply-and-demand backdrop deserves. Both point to a steeper curve.

## The trade

### Choosing the expression

The view has two legs, a front end that rallies relative to forecasts and a long end that cheapens, so I want a curve trade rather than a bet on the level of yields.

**Which part of the curve?**

| Spread | Driven by | Fit with the thesis | 2026 path | Hot-CPI exposure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2s10s | The Fed path | Strong on the front end, partial on the long end | 59 → 20 → 47bp | Highest |
| **5s30s** | **Term premium and supply** | **Strong on the long end; front end still captured by the 5Y** | **113 → 41 → 60bp** | **Lower** |
| 2s30s | Both | Both legs | 126 → 53 → 82bp | High |

I pick **5s30s**. Most of my arguments (corporate supply, Japan, term premium, Fed independence) bite at the 10–30 year sector. The 5-year still captures "fewer hikes than priced". It is less hostage to a single inflation print than the 2-year, and it has the most ground to recover.

**Which instrument?**

| Instrument | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Treasury futures (long FV, short WN)** | **Exchange-traded, liquid, transparent; standard tool for curve trades** | **Hedge ratio depends on the cheapest-to-deliver bond** |
| Cash Treasuries | Exactly the bonds in the thesis | Shorting the 30-year requires repo access |
| SOFR swaps | Clean exposure to rate expectations | Misses the Treasury-specific supply story, which is half the thesis |
| Options (e.g. SOFR call spreads) | Defined maximum loss | Only cover the front-end leg; that trade is already crowded; premium costs carry |
| ETF pair (short- vs long-duration funds) | Accessible | Borrow costs; imprecise duration matching |

Futures win. The swaps row matters most to me: part of my view is that Treasuries specifically have too much supply, so a swap curve trade would partly hedge away the thing I am trying to own.

### Making it a pure curve bet: DV01-neutral

A 30-year bond moves far more for a 1bp change in yield than a 5-year note. At current yields, $1 million of the 30-year gains or loses about $1,436 per basis point (its **DV01**), against about $437 for the 5-year. If I bought and sold equal amounts, a parallel sell-off would make me money through the short 30-year leg even if the curve didn't steepen at all. I'd really be holding a bet on rising yields.

So I balance the legs by DV01, which is about 3.3 times as much 5-year as 30-year in cash terms, or roughly 5.5–7 FV contracts per WN contract in futures. The exact ratio depends on which bond is cheapest to deliver into the Ultra bond contract, and I take it from CME's published figures at entry. With equal DV01 on each leg, parallel moves cancel, and the P&L depends only on the spread:

$$
\text{P\&L} \approx \Delta(\text{5s30s, in bp}) \times \text{DV01 per leg}
$$

### Entry by scaling in around CPI

The curve has already steepened 19bp off its September low in two weeks, and September CPI is out on 14 October. I expect a hot headline print, as AAA's national average gas price rose from $4.14 to $4.48 a gallon through September, and core CPI beat expectations in August. A hot print would push the front end higher and flatten the curve, at least for a few days.

So I scale in:

- **Tranche 1 (half size):** entered at the 5s30s close on 60bp (5 October close).
- **Tranche 2 (half size):** a limit order at **52bp**, valid until the 28 October FOMC. If it doesn't fill, I stay half-sized.

### Levels and sizing

All levels use the daily close of 5s30s computed from the U.S. Treasury's published par yield curve.

- **Stop: 39bp.** That is 2bp below the 23 September low of 41bp. A new low would mean the turn in the curve I'm betting on hasn't happened. I considered a tighter stop under the more recent higher low (50bp on 28 September), but a hot CPI print, which I expect, could easily push the curve through 50 without breaking the thesis. 5s30s moves about 2–3bp on a typical day and up to 7bp, so a stop just below my 52bp add level would mostly measure noise.
- **Target: 90bp.** That is back to the mid-August level, before the September Fed repricing flattened the curve.
- **1R = $100,000**, or 10bp of a hypothetical $100 million book.

Sizing takes two steps. First, the risk budget sets the position DV01: how many dollars each tranche makes or loses per basis point of 5s30s. I want a stop-out with both tranches on to cost exactly 1R. Tranche 1 risks 60 − 39 = 21bp and tranche 2 risks 52 − 39 = 13bp, so each dollar of position DV01 puts 34bp at risk:

$$
\text{Position DV01 per tranche} = \frac{\$100{,}000}{34\text{bp}} \approx \$2{,}941 \text{ per bp}
$$

Second, each bond's own DV01 converts that into notional. A $1 million position carries about $437 per bp in the 5-year and $1,436 in the 30-year:

$$
\frac{\$2{,}941}{\$437} \approx \$6.7\text{mm of 5-year (long)} \qquad \frac{\$2{,}941}{\$1{,}436} \approx \$2.0\text{mm of 30-year (short)}
$$

In futures the same division uses each contract's DV01, roughly $43 for FV and $265 for WN, which gives about 68 FV contracts against 11 WN per tranche. The WN contract is far more sensitive than $100,000 of the cash 30-year because it tracks a long, low-coupon cheapest-to-deliver bond, scaled by its conversion factor.

![Payoff of the steepener in R](/images/2026-10-5s30s-payoff.svg "P&L in R at each 5s30s level. Every 34bp of total movement across the tranches is worth 1R.")

| 5s30s close | Both tranches | Tranche 1 only |
|---|---|---|
| 39 (stop) | −1.00R | −0.62R |
| 44 | −0.71R | −0.47R |
| 52 | −0.24R | −0.24R |
| 60 | +0.24R | 0.00R |
| 75 | +1.12R | +0.44R |
| 90 (target) | +2.00R | +0.88R |

Reward to risk is about **2.0 : 1** with both tranches on and 1.4 : 1 with only the first.

> **Primer: R.** Measuring results in R (multiples of the amount I risk) rather than dollars makes ideas comparable. A +2R win on a small position and a +2R win on a big one show the same quality of decision. It also forces the stop to be decided *before* the position, because the stop defines what 1R is.

**Carry.** The trade is paid to wait. Financed at about 4%, the long 5-year earns roughly 24bp a year in yield terms and the short 30-year costs roughly 12bp, for net carry of about +13bp a year on the spread. On the full position that is worth around 0.19R over three months if nothing moves.

## Risks and what would prove me wrong

**Price rules**

- **Stop:** a close at or below 39bp. I close everything.
- **Protect a winner:** if 5s30s closes at or above 75bp, I move the stop to break-even (56bp, the average entry, with both tranches on; 60bp with only tranche 1).
- **Time stop:** if neither stop nor target is hit, I close on Friday 18 December and write the post-mortem. If the curve hasn't moved by the week after the December FOMC, the catalysts I'm counting on will have passed.

**Thesis triggers when I exit even if the price stop hasn't been hit**

- **Treasury cuts long-end supply.** At the 4 November refunding, Treasury cuts 20- or 30-year auction sizes, or makes long-end buybacks larger and regular. In August it guided that coupon sizes would stay unchanged for at least several quarters, but later wording opened the door to fewer long-dated sales. This is the biggest risk to the trade because it attacks the short leg directly.
- **The Fed proves more hawkish than I think.** If it hikes on 28 October, I cancel the tranche 2 order and reassess. If the December dots show more than one additional hike in 2027 (a median above 4.375%), I close since the Fed would then agree with market pricing.
- **Core inflation re-accelerates.** If core CPI prints 0.4% or higher in both the September (14 October) and October (10 November) reports, I close. A hot headline print driven by gasoline alone is not a trigger, and it is why tranche 2 exists.

**Watch items**

- An Iran truce and a collapse in oil. It would likely lower front-end yields (good for the trade) but also long-run inflation fears (bad), so the net effect is unclear.
- The 3 November midterms. A divided Congress would likely mean less fiscal expansion and a lower risk premium in long bonds.
- Term premium falling back toward 0.5%.
- A strong rebound in October payrolls.
